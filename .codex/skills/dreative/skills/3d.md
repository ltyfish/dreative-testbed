# 3D

Use 3D when the subject is spatial — when turning it, lighting it, cutting
through it, or standing inside it is how a person understands the thing. A
subject fully explained by a good photograph is not spatial, and a renderer
will make it worse. A subject whose whole point is how its parts sit in space
is spatial, and no photograph will do the job.

Both halves of that are real. This file is not an argument against 3D.

## Where the object comes from

Decide before choosing a runtime. This is a difficulty ranking, not a
preference ranking — each step down costs more and is easier to get visibly
wrong, which is a reason to know which step you are on, not a reason to always
take the first.

1. **A licensed or CC0 model.** Poly Haven, Sketchfab's downloadable CC
   licences, or a supplied asset. See `../references/MEDIA_SOURCES.md`. For a
   realistic physical subject this is usually the right answer and rarely the
   one that gets chosen.
2. **A photograph, used as a photograph.** If the object only needs to be seen
   rather than turned, a well-cropped image beats a mediocre render.
3. **Modelled or reconstructed geometry.** The right answer for schematics,
   data objects, abstract and generated forms, and anything not pretending to
   be a photograph. Much of the most striking work on the web lives here.
4. **Procedural DOM/CSS/SVG built to look dimensional.** A legitimate graphic
   style; an illegitimate stand-in for a real object. If it is meant to read as
   a real thing and doesn't, it failed regardless of how much markup it took.
   This is entry 10 in `../exemplars/SLOP.md`.

Photo-to-3D reconstruction is not an available capability. Photogrammetry needs
controlled captures you do not have, and image-to-3D services return unreliable
geometry on exactly the detailed subjects that would justify using them. Raise
it as a capability gap; never attempt it quietly.

## Lighting and material are the work

This is where a technically working scene still looks worse than the flat
design it replaced. An unlit glTF on a gradient is the 3D equivalent of a stock
hero.

- **The environment does most of the job.** One CC0 HDRI with a considered
  exposure beats three point lights, every time.
- **Sample colour from the real material,** not from the page's accent. A
  reviewer notices invented colour long before they notice geometry — *"the
  colour choice is weird for props"* is a lighting note, not a modelling one.
- **Give the object a ground relationship.** Contact shadow, reflection, or a
  surface. A floating object with no shadow is the clearest tell that nobody
  art-directed the scene.
- **Check silhouette and scale at 390px,** where a subject that read at 1440px
  becomes an ambiguous blob.

## Choosing live or pre-rendered

These are two different mediums, and the choice is a design decision worth
making deliberately rather than defaulting either way.

**Pre-rendered frames** — a turntable or process sequence scrubbed on scroll,
authored views crossfaded, a single well-lit still. The output is fixed at
author time where you can inspect it, so it is much harder to get visibly
wrong, and it is cheap and reliable on phones. Recipes in
`../references/MEDIA_SOURCES.md`.

**A live scene** — when the reader genuinely moves the object, configures it,
needs a viewpoint you cannot predict, or when the material itself is the point
and must respond to real light and real input. Shaders, generative geometry,
and instanced systems belong here too: their whole value is that they are
computed rather than filmed, and pre-rendering them throws that away.

Neither is the safe choice. Pre-rendering a subject the reader wanted to hold
is as much a failure as shipping a live scene of an object that never moves.

## Naming what shipped

Say honestly what it is: model, layered billboard, media plane, procedural
geometry, frame sequence, or pre-rendered view. A textured plane is not a
model; a stepped image sequence is not continuous subject motion.

Browser checks confirm a canvas exists, is visible, is large enough, and changes
state. None of that says whether the object reads as the thing it is meant to
be. That judgement is the user's — show the render and ask, and never report a
passing smoke run as evidence the prop looks right.

## Runtime discipline

Cap DPR, lazy load, suspend offscreen, handle resize and context loss, dispose
every geometry, material, texture, and renderer. Provide a lightweight fallback
and an authored mobile form that preserves the product view or explanation
rather than degrading to a generic gradient.

## How this fails

Generic particles standing in for an idea. Decorative objects floating because
the scene needed filling. A canvas that persists over copy and controls.
Complexity the reader cannot perceive, paid for in load time. And the failure
this file has historically caused: reading all of the above as *don't*, and
shipping a flat page for a subject that was spatial the whole time.
