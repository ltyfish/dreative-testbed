# Cinematic

Use when the subject has an order — when understanding it means being shown
things in a sequence, and the sequence is the argument.

Cinema's actual tools are pacing, framing, and the cut. Not video. A page can be
cinematic in still type and photography and no motion at all, and a page with a
looping hero film usually isn't.

## The sequence has to be about something

Before pacing anything, be able to say what the reader knows at the end that
they did not know at the start, and which beats got them there. Setup,
escalation, peak, rest, resolution is the usual shape, but it is a shape you
should be able to justify against this subject rather than lay over it.

Then decide what carries each cut: media, type, colour, scale, or the subject's
own state. Letting them hand off together is what makes a change of section feel
like a change of scene instead of a scroll position.

## The reader is holding the projector

This is the difference from film, and every real problem comes from it. They
can leave, skim, arrive in the middle, come back, or want the phone number
immediately. Navigation, focus, reduced motion, and a way out of a long
sequence are not concessions that weaken the piece — a sequence that survives
being skipped through is simply better built than one that doesn't.

## How this fails

Long pinned scenes that hold the reader still while a little text changes.
Waiting presented as pacing. Constant motion, which flattens into no motion,
because nothing can be a peak when everything is. Transitions that stand
between the reader and the product. And the quiet one: a route with correct
five-act structure and nothing at stake in any act, which is what happens when
the shape got applied before the argument existed.
